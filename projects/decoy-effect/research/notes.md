# The decoy effect (asymmetric dominance): research notes (2026-10-19)

The claims and URLs are in `sources.json` (c1-c16). Format: 45s, 9:16 Instagram business reel.

## The one-sentence idea
Add an option that is clearly worse than the one you want people to pick, but only slightly cheaper or the same price. The target then looks like a bargain by comparison. (Academic name: "asymmetrically dominated alternative", Huber, Payne & Puto, *Journal of Consumer Research*, 1982.)

## Verified numbers (use exactly)
- **The Economist offer (as Ariely reports it):** web-only **$59**, print-only **$125**, print + web **$125**.
- **With the decoy (100 MIT students):** **16** web-only, **0** print-only, **84** print + web.
- **Without the decoy (a separate 100 students):** **68** web-only, **32** print + web.
- Sources: Ariely's TED talk transcript (EG 2008), where he says "I gave it to 100 MIT students" and then "another 100 students", and his book *Predictably Irrational* (2008), ch. 1.
- **Real-world size:** in **3.6 million** UK supermarket wine purchases, decoys shifted choice by **roughly 1%**, and less for frequent buyers (npj Science of Learning, Aug 2025).
- **Lab average:** about a **14.7%** share gain for the target in a meta-analysis (Yang, Cornell PhD, 2013).

## Recommended phrasing for the Economist example
> "In a classroom demo described in his 2008 book *Predictably Irrational*, Dan Ariely showed 100 MIT students three Economist offers: web $59, print $125, print + web $125. 84 chose the bundle. When he removed the print-only option, a different group of 100 split 68 to 32 the other way."

Always frame it as **"a classroom demo" or "Ariely reports"**. Don't say "a study proved" or "The Economist's sales data".
Caption or pinned-comment caveat: "Hypothetical choices, not real sales. Later replications were mixed. Ariely's separate 2012 and 2002 papers were retracted over data problems (2021, 2026). This demo hasn't been accused of fraud, but it was never peer-reviewed."

## Caveats (must follow)
1. **Not a peer-reviewed study.** The Economist demo is a hypothetical classroom choice reported in a book and a TED talk. There is no published dataset.
2. **Replication is shaky.** The Economist scenario failed to replicate in Yang & Lynn (2014). Kaptein et al. (*Palgrave Communications*, 2016, N = 7,125) say the original decoy was "ill-positioned". They found the effect appears only once the decoy's price is moved.
3. **Ariely's credibility.** PNAS retracted his 2012 car-insurance "sign at the top" honesty paper in Sept 2021 (fabricated data, per Data Colada). *Psychological Science* retracted his 2002 procrastination/deadlines paper on 2 Sept 2026. **Neither involves the Economist demo**, so don't imply it was faked. But don't lean on Ariely as an authority either. Credit the 1982 Huber, Payne & Puto paper as the real origin.
4. **Popcorn ($3 / $6.50 / $7) is illustrative only.** It isn't from Ariely or his TED talk. Retellings credit a vague "National Geographic Channel" segment, and I found no data. If you use it, say "Picture a cinema menu..." and don't say "a study found".
5. **Williams-Sonoma bread maker is a REPORTED anecdote.** Simonson (*California Management Review*, 1993) reports that sales of the cheaper machine "roughly doubled" after a pricier model was added. The $275 / $429 figures come from pop retellings that vary ($279, $415). On screen, use "reportedly nearly doubled" and avoid exact prices, or say "about $275 vs. a ~$400+ model".
6. **Apple storage tiers and Starbucks sizes are interpretations, not evidence.** If shown, say "looks like a decoy", never "Apple uses a decoy to...".
7. **Size and limits.** It works on numeric, side-by-side comparisons and on buyers who don't already know what they want. It fades when people taste, see or experience the product (Frederick, Lee & Baskin, JMR 2014) and among frequent buyers (wine study). Don't promise "double your sales".

## How-to (for the CTA segment)
1. Pick your **target** (the plan you actually want to sell).
2. Add a **decoy** that is close to the target but clearly worse, e.g. the same price with fewer features, or nearly the same price with much less.
3. Make the comparison **numeric and side by side**, as on a pricing table.
4. **A/B test** the decoy's price and position. Small changes swing the effect (Kaptein et al. 2016).
5. **Ethics:** the decoy must be real and purchasable at that price. Don't use fake "was" prices (FTC 16 CFR 233.1), fake scarcity or hidden fees in the target tier. Your target should still be good value. The decoy only makes the value easier to see.
