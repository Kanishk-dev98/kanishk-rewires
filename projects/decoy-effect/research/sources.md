- **c4** (caveat, event n/a): The Economist scenario failed to replicate in Yang & Lynn (2014). Kaptein, Van Emden & Iannuzzi (2016, N=7,125 MTurk) list it among scenarios that 'previously failed to replicate' and conclude the original decoy was 'ill-positioned'. They found the effect appears when the decoy is moved, so the result depends on the exact numbers used. Their version used web $56.95 / print $143.90 / print+web $125.

- **c8** (fact, event 1982-06): Original academic definition: Huber, J., Payne, J. W., & Puto, C. (1982). Adding Asymmetrically Dominated Alternatives: Violations of Regularity and the Similarity Hypothesis. Journal of Consumer Research, 9(1), 90-98. doi:10.1086/208899. Finding: adding an option that is worse than one alternative in every respect (but not worse than the other) raises the share of the option that dominates it. This violates 'regularity', the rule that adding options can't increase an existing option's share.

- **c13** (caveat, event 2014-08): Limits of the effect: Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014) found the attraction effect is largely confined to stylized choices where every attribute is a number. It usually vanishes when people experience the product (e.g., taste a drink) or when even one attribute is shown perceptually. In the same issue, Yang & Lynn report that only 11 of 91 attempts (23 product classes, 73 choice sets) produced reliable effects. Huber, Payne & Puto replied ('Let's Be Honest About the Attraction Effect') that the effect is robust when the original conditions are replicated, and strongest when preferences are not yet formed.

- **c14** (stat, event 2013-05-26): A meta-analysis in Sybil Yang's 2013 Cornell dissertation estimates an average share gain of about 14.7% for the dominating target in lab studies. The effect is weaker when the target is already popular, and 'methodological artifacts may have exaggerated' its strength. The 2025 field study found about 1% in real purchases. Takeaway: real, but much smaller and more fragile outside the lab than pop-psych claims.

- **c15** (fact, event n/a): How-to, drawn from the literature. (1) The decoy must be clearly worse than your target on every attribute, but not clearly worse than the competing option. It should sit close to the target, e.g. similar price with fewer features. (2) It works best with numeric, easy-to-compare attributes and with buyers whose preferences are unformed. It works poorly with experienced or visual attributes and with expert or frequent buyers. (3) The decoy's exact position matters a lot, so A/B test where you place it rather than copying the Economist numbers.

- **c16** (caveat, event n/a): Ethics and law: the decoy must be a real, purchasable offer at a true price. A fictitious 'was' or reference price is deceptive under FTC rules: if the former price is 'not bona fide but fictitious', the bargain is 'a false one' (16 CFR 233.1). The FTC's 2022 staff report treats interface designs that manipulate purchase decisions (fake scarcity, fake timers) as dark patterns it will pursue. Don't hide fees in the target tier or fake the decoy's features.
